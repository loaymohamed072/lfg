## How to use this file
This is the "physics engine" of every LFG program. Every template, progression, and adaptation in files 02–07 is downstream of these principles. When a client's plan feels wrong, diagnose it here first: is a principle being violated (too little overload, too much fatigue, wrong specificity)?

The principles in priority order (Helms' hierarchy, adapted): **1) Adherence & individualization → 2) Specificity → 3) Overload → 4) Progression → 5) Fatigue management & recovery → 6) Volume/intensity/frequency dosing.** A perfectly optimized program the client won't do beats nothing, and loses to a "good enough" program they do for two years.

## 1. Specificity (SAID principle)
**Specific Adaptations to Imposed Demands.** The body adapts to exactly what you make it do — the movement pattern, the load, the speed, the energy system, the range of motion.

Practical rules the AI copilot must encode:
- **Train the goal.** Want a bigger squat? Squat. Want to run a faster 5k? Run at and around 5k effort. Want fat loss? The training's job is to retain muscle and add energy expenditure, not to "burn fat" via any magic exercise.
- **Transfer is proportional to similarity.** An exercise carries over to a goal to the degree it shares the movement pattern, load, and velocity. Leg press builds legs but transfers less to a squat 1RM than squatting does.
- **Energy-system specificity.** Aerobic base is built by sustained sub-threshold work; VO2max by hard 3–5 min intervals; strength-endurance (HYROX) by exactly the compromised-running/station patterns of the event. See files 05–06.
- **Specificity has a cost:** the more specific you get, the narrower the adaptation. Beginners should stay general (broad movement competence); advanced athletes narrow toward their goal as the event approaches.

**Heuristic:** the closer the competition/goal, the more specific the training. Off-season = general and varied; pre-event = specific and rehearsal-like.

## 2. Overload
Adaptation requires a stimulus **above** what the body is currently accustomed to. No overload, no change — the body has no reason to adapt.

Overload can be applied by increasing any of these (the "progression levers"):
1. **Load** (more weight on the bar)
2. **Reps** (more reps at a given load)
3. **Sets / volume** (more total work)
4. **Frequency** (more sessions per week)
5. **Density** (same work in less rest / less time)
6. **Range of motion / tension quality** (fuller ROM, more control, better mind-muscle connection on isolation)
7. **Proximity to failure** (training closer to true failure, i.e. fewer reps in reserve)

**Rule:** change ONE primary lever at a time so you can read the response. For strength, load is king. For hypertrophy, load-or-reps first, then volume over mesocycles. For endurance, weekly volume/duration is the master lever, then intensity.

**Overload must be relative to the individual.** A "hard" set for a beginner and an advanced lifter are different absolute loads but the same relative effort.

## 3. Progression
Progression is overload applied **systematically over time**. Without a progression model, sessions drift and the client plateaus or overreaches. The AI must always attach a progression rule to a program (models detailed in file 03).

Core progression models (choose by training age and goal):
- **Linear progression (LP):** add a small fixed load each session/week. Best for true beginners (novice effect). E.g. +2.5 kg upper / +5 kg lower per session on main lifts until it stalls.
- **Double progression:** work within a rep range at a fixed load; when you hit the top of the range for all sets, add load and drop back to the bottom of the range. Best default for intermediates and most isolation work. E.g. 3×8–12; when 3×12 is achieved, add load, return to ~8.
- **Volume progression (mesocycle):** start a block near MEV and add ~1–3 sets/muscle/week, riding fatigue up to near MRV, then deload. RP model. Best for hypertrophy blocks.
- **Autoregulated (RPE/RIR):** load/volume is set by daily readiness against a target effort (e.g. work up to a top set at RPE 8, then back-off sets). Best for intermediate–advanced and anyone with variable schedules/stress. See file 02.

**When to advance vs. hold:** advance when the current session was completed at or above target quality (reps hit, technique clean, RIR at or above target). Hold or repeat when reps were missed, RIR was lower than planned (grinding), or technique degraded.

**When progress stalls:** in order — (a) confirm recovery/sleep/nutrition, (b) deload one week, (c) reduce to a lower rep/higher-effort scheme or swap the progression model, (d) only then question the exercise selection or volume.

## 4. Fatigue Management
Adaptation = **stimulus − fatigue** managed over time. Fatigue is the tax on the stimulus: too little and you undertrain; too much and fatigue masks fitness and injury risk climbs. This is the RP "Stimulus-to-Fatigue Ratio" (SFR) idea and Helms' fatigue-management layer.

Key ideas to encode:
- **Stimulus-to-Fatigue Ratio (SFR):** prefer exercises that give high muscle stimulus for low systemic/joint fatigue. A machine chest press or a hack squat often has a better SFR than max-effort barbell work for a general-fitness/fat-loss client. Reserve the high-fatigue lifts for people whose goal is those lifts.
- **Fatigue accumulates faster than fitness dissipates.** You lose the day-to-day fatigue in ~5–10 days (a deload), but fitness lingers weeks — which is why deloads work.
- **Signs of excess fatigue:** performance drops session to session, resting HR elevated, sleep/appetite/mood off, motivation crashes, nagging joints, cycle irregularities in some women. Bar speed slowing at a fixed load is an objective early flag.
- **Deload:** a planned ~1-week reduction in volume (drop ~40–60% of sets) and/or intensity (drop ~10–20% load, keep some speed) every 4–8 weeks, or when accumulated fatigue is masking fitness. Deloads are not "wasted" weeks — they are where you actually realize the adaptation you built.

**Heuristic:** if two sessions in a row got worse, not better, and nutrition/sleep are fine, you are in fatigue debt — deload or reduce volume, don't push harder.

## 5. Volume, Intensity, Frequency (the dosing triad)
These are the three dials the AI turns to prescribe a dose. They interact: raising one usually forces lowering another.

### Volume (how much)
- Counted as **hard sets per muscle group per week** for hypertrophy; **weekly minutes/km** for endurance.
- **Hypertrophy landmarks (RP model + Schoenfeld dose-response):**
  - **MV (Maintenance):** ~4–6 sets/muscle/week — holds muscle in a deficit or busy phase.
  - **MEV (Minimum Effective):** ~8–10 sets/muscle/week — the floor for growth.
  - **MAV (Maximum Adaptive):** ~10–20 sets/muscle/week — where most growth happens for most people.
  - **MRV (Maximum Recoverable):** ~20+ sets/muscle/week — the ceiling; sustainable only briefly before a deload.
  - Evidence (Schoenfeld et al.): ~10+ weekly sets beat lower volumes; benefits taper and recovery cost rises at very high volumes. Start intermediates at ~10–12 and earn more.
- **Beginners need less** (MEV can be 6–8 sets and they grow) and **should not start at MRV** — they'd only accumulate junk fatigue.

### Intensity (how hard)
- **Intensity of load** = % of 1RM / rep range. **Intensity of effort** = proximity to failure (RIR/RPE).
- Muscle grows across a wide load range (~30–85% 1RM) **if sets are taken close to failure** (~0–4 RIR). Strength needs heavier loads (≥80% 1RM, ~1–5 reps) trained at higher relative intensity but rarely to failure.
- Most hypertrophy work sits at **1–3 RIR**; most strength work at **1–4 RIR** on top sets. True failure is a tool used sparingly (last set of an isolation, not on compound barbell lifts).

### Frequency (how often)
- **Per muscle:** ≥2×/week is modestly better than 1× for hypertrophy **when volume is matched** (Schoenfeld frequency meta-analysis) — mostly because it lets you distribute volume with better per-session quality. Frequency is a tool to fit volume in, not a magic variable itself.
- **Per lift (strength):** more frequent practice of a heavy skill (squat/bench/deadlift) drives strength via skill acquisition — 2–4×/week per lift for dedicated strength athletes.
- **Whole-body sessions/week:** 3–5 for most general-fitness clients; the split (file 02) then distributes muscles across those days.

**Interaction rule:** total weekly volume is the primary driver; frequency and per-session volume are just how you package it. Don't chase frequency at the cost of recovery or adherence.

## 6. Individualization
Same principles, different people. The AI must adjust the dose to the person, not force the person into a template.

Individualize on:
- **Training age / status:** beginner (linear gains, low volume, high skill focus) → intermediate (needs periodization, more volume, autoregulation) → advanced (needs specificity, high volume tolerance, tight fatigue management).
- **Recovery capacity:** sleep, stress, age, nutrition, life load. A stressed executive client in a deficit recovers less than a 22-year-old on maintenance calories — same program, different appropriate volume.
- **Anatomy / injury history / leverages:** exercise selection bends to the joints and history in front of you (file 07). There is no mandatory exercise; there are only movement patterns that must be trained, via whatever variation the client tolerates.
- **Preferences & adherence:** the client's enjoyment is a performance variable. Two effective options → pick the one they'll do consistently.
- **Female-specific factors:** covered in file 07. Current evidence does not support mandatory cycle-phase periodization of strength; individualize around symptoms, not a rigid phase template.

**Heuristic:** program to the client's *current recoverable* volume, not the volume you wish they could handle. Earn volume; don't front-load it.

## 7. Recovery
Training breaks the body down; recovery is when adaptation is actually built. Programming that ignores recovery is just organized fatigue.

The recovery hierarchy (highest leverage first):
1. **Sleep** — 7–9 h. The single biggest recovery lever; deficits blunt hypertrophy, strength, and fat loss and raise injury risk.
2. **Nutrition** — adequate protein (≥1.6 g/kg, up to ~2.2–2.4 g/kg in a deficit; file 04), enough total energy, carbs to fuel hard sessions, hydration (relevant in Dubai heat).
3. **Managed stress load** — psychological stress competes for the same recovery budget as training.
4. **Programmed recovery** — deloads, easy days, and the easy 80% of endurance volume (file 05) that most people skip.
5. **Active recovery / mobility** — low-intensity movement, walking, and targeted mobility for problem joints; useful but secondary to sleep and nutrition.

**Rule the AI enforces:** never add training stress to solve a recovery problem. If the client is under-recovered, fix sleep/nutrition/stress or reduce load — do not "train through it" with more volume.

## Quick decision heuristics (cheat sheet)
- **Goal unclear → clarify before programming.** Every principle bends to the goal (specificity).
- **Beginner → linear progression, low volume (MEV), high skill/technique focus, full-body 3×/week.**
- **Progress stalled + recovery fine → deload, then change one lever.**
- **Two sessions worse in a row → fatigue debt → back off.**
- **Choosing an exercise → highest stimulus-to-fatigue ratio the client can do pain-free and repeat.**
- **Choosing a dose → start at the low end of the effective range and progress; volume is earned.**
- **Adherence risk → simplify. A program done beats an optimal program skipped.**
