# 08 — Pelvic Floor & Women's Health (coach-level basics)

> Source: Association of Chartered Physiotherapists in Women's Health (ACPWH) patient leaflet, "Pelvic Floor Muscle Exercises." Original synthesis for coaching context. **Scope: this is general education, not treatment. Pelvic health is a clinical area — refer to a specialist women's-health physiotherapist for any symptoms.**

Relevant to a largely-female clientele (postpartum, perimenopause/menopause, runners, lifters). A coach should understand the basics, screen for it sensitively, and refer out. A coach does not diagnose or rehab pelvic floor dysfunction.

---

## Why a coach should care

The pelvic floor muscles span the base of the pelvis and support the bladder, bowel, and uterus. They need to be both strong and well-timed. Dysfunction is common and often silently limits training, especially high-impact running and heavy lifting.

**Risk/trigger factors:** pregnancy and childbirth, menopause, being significantly overweight, chronic constipation/straining, chronic cough. Symptoms are common but are **not** an unavoidable part of ageing.

## Screen for it (privately, without pressure)

Ask, in intake or when programming impact work, whether the client ever experiences:
- Leaking urine when coughing, laughing, sneezing, jumping, or running.
- Sudden urgency or leaking on the way to the toilet.
- Any bowel-control issues.
- A feeling of heaviness or "something coming down" (possible prolapse).
- Discomfort or leakage during sex.

Handle answers with total discretion and zero embarrassment. Any "yes" → **recommend a women's-health physiotherapist** before loading heavy impact/lifting, and modify programming in the meantime (lower impact, manage intra-abdominal pressure, avoid breath-holding under max load).

## The exercise basics (what clients can safely do)

The correct action is a "lift and squeeze," as if stopping wind and urine at once, without clenching glutes or legs and while breathing normally. A gentle lower-abdominal co-contraction is normal.

A standard programme trains both:
- **Long holds:** contract, hold up to ~10 seconds, relax for several seconds, repeat until the muscles tire.
- **Short/quick squeezes:** one-second contractions, repeat until fatigue.

Typical target is around 10 long holds + 10 short squeezes, at least 3 times a day, starting seated or lying and progressing to standing. It is not a quick fix; improvement usually shows over **3-5 months** of consistent practice, and it fits Ahmed's "make it a daily habit like brushing your teeth" framing.

**Two safety notes to pass on:** if the client sees any downward bulging when they contract, stop and see a specialist (it can worsen a prolapse); and do not practise by repeatedly stopping urine mid-stream.

## Coaching integration

- **Pre-brace for load:** teach clients to gently engage the pelvic floor before lifting, coughing, or sneezing.
- **Address the modifiable drivers:** managing bodyweight and constipation (fibre, hydration) reduces strain, which dovetails with the nutrition plan.
- **Postpartum:** treat return-to-impact as a graded, physio-guided process, not a fixed timeline (see `07_special_populations_and_injuries.md`).

## Hard guardrail

Coaches educate and refer; they do not treat pelvic-floor dysfunction, prolapse, or incontinence. Persistent or significant symptoms always route to a specialist women's-health physiotherapist.
