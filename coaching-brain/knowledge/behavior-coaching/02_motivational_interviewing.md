# Motivational Interviewing for Coaches

> Sources: Miller & Rollnick *Motivational Interviewing* (MI spirit, OARS, change talk/sustain talk, rolling with discord); SAMHSA TIP-35 MI counseling guidance; current MI evidence in health-behavior change (change-talk vs sustain-talk outcome research). Original explanations, no verbatim text. **Scope note:** MI is a communication style for coaching, not therapy. For clinical depression, eating disorders, or trauma, use MI's warmth to *refer out* (see file 05), not to treat.

---

## 0. How the copilot should use this file

MI is the tool for **any client who is ambivalent, resistant, stuck, "not motivated," pushing back, or being told what to do isn't working.** The core insight: people are far more persuaded by their *own* reasons for change than by yours. So the coach's job is not to lecture, convince, or install motivation — it's to *evoke the client's existing motivation* and let them argue for change themselves.

The single biggest mistake this file prevents: the **"righting reflex"** — the coach's urge to fix, warn, or correct ("you just need to eat less and move more"). It reliably triggers the client to defend the status quo. When a client argues *against* change, MI theory says the coach is usually causing it.

> **Master rule for the copilot:** When a client is resistant, tell the coach to *stop persuading and start asking.* Talk less, reflect more, and let the client voice the reasons to change.

---

## 1. The Spirit of MI (this matters more than the techniques)

MI only works if the underlying stance is right. Techniques without the spirit feel manipulative and fail. Four elements:

- **Partnership** — coach and client are collaborators, not expert-and-patient. You work *with* her, not *on* her.
- **Acceptance** — respect her autonomy (it's her choice), affirm her strengths, and show accurate empathy. No judgment.
- **Compassion** — you're acting in her best interest, not to hit your own agenda or ego.
- **Evocation** — draw out what's already inside her (her values, goals, reasons) rather than installing what you think should be there.

This maps directly onto SDT (file 01): MI is essentially autonomy-supportive coaching in conversation form.

## 2. OARS — the four core skills

The practical toolkit. These are what the coach actually *does* in a conversation.

### O — Open questions
Questions that can't be answered yes/no; they open the client up and generate change talk. Aim for more open than closed questions.
- ❌ "Did you do your workouts?" → ✅ "How did training go for you this week?"
- ❌ "Do you want to lose weight?" → ✅ "What would getting stronger change for you?"

### A — Affirmations
Genuine recognition of effort, strength, or intention (not empty praise). Builds competence and relatedness (SDT), and buffers the ego so the client can be honest.
- "You showed up three times even with a brutal work week — that takes real commitment."
- "You're being honest about what's hard. That's exactly what makes this work."

### R — Reflective listening
The heart of MI. You say back what you heard — sometimes the literal content, sometimes the underlying feeling or meaning. Reflection shows you get it, keeps her talking, and *selectively amplifies change talk.*
- **Simple reflection:** "So the mornings just haven't been working."
- **Complex reflection (adds meaning):** "It sounds like it's less about the exercise and more about feeling like you've lost control of your schedule."
- **Amplified/one-sided reflection** (use gently to evoke the other side): "So there's really no part of this that's worth it to you." — often prompts her to argue *for* change: "Well, no, I do want to feel strong again…"

> Reflections should outnumber questions. A good ratio is roughly 2 reflections per question. The copilot should nudge coaches who are interrogating ("you asked 5 questions in a row — reflect what she said before asking again").

### S — Summaries
Periodically gather what she's said, especially collecting the change talk into one place, then hand it back. This makes her hear her own case for change, stacked.
- "Let me pull this together: you're frustrated the scale stalled, you loved how you felt after the 5k, you want to keep up with your kids, *and* you're worried you can't fit training around work. Did I get that right?"

## 3. Change talk vs. sustain talk — the thing to actually listen for

Every client statement leans one of two ways. Current MI research is clear: **more change talk (and stronger change talk) predicts behavior change; more sustain talk predicts staying stuck.** The coach's real-time job is to *evoke, reflect, and reinforce change talk* and *not amplify* sustain talk.

**Change talk** — the client's own language *for* change. Remember it with **DARN-CAT**:
- **D**esire — "I want to feel fit again."
- **A**bility — "I could probably manage two mornings."
- **R**easons — "My blood pressure scared me."
- **N**eed — "I have to fix this before the wedding."
- **C**ommitment — "I'm going to train Monday." *(strongest — predicts action)*
- **A**ctivation — "I'm ready to start."
- **T**aking steps — "I already booked the class."

**Sustain talk** — language *for* the status quo: "I've never been able to stick to anything," "I don't have time," "diets don't work for me." Don't argue with it and don't reflect it in a way that deepens it. Acknowledge it, then steer back toward change talk.

### How to *evoke* change talk (ready-to-use)
1. **Ask evocative questions** (see scripts in §6): "Why might you want to make this change?" "What worries you if nothing changes?"
2. **Ask for elaboration:** "Tell me more about that." "What else?"
3. **Use scaling questions** (below).
4. **Look back / look forward:** "What was different when you were training consistently before?" / "If we nailed this, what does life look like in six months?"
5. **Explore values:** "What matters most to you as a person?" then connect the goal to it.

### Scaling questions (the copilot's favorite MI micro-tool)
- "On a scale of 0–10, how important is this to you right now?" → she says 6.
- **The key follow-up (never skip):** "Why a 6 and not a 3?" → this forces her to voice change talk. *Never* ask "why not a 9?" — that evokes sustain talk.
- Same for confidence: "How confident are you, 0–10, that you can do 3 sessions this week?" → "Why a 7 and not a 4?" → then "What would move you from a 7 to an 8?"

## 4. Rolling with resistance / discord — don't wrestle

When a client pushes back, gets defensive, or argues, MI's stance is **do not oppose it head-on.** Arguing creates more arguing; the client digs in. Modern MI reframes "resistance" as either **sustain talk** (ambivalence about the behavior) or **discord** (a strain in the *relationship* — she feels judged, unheard, or pressured). Treat them differently:

- **Sustain talk** → keep using OARS, evoke the other side of the ambivalence.
- **Discord** ("you don't understand my life," "this isn't working") → repair the *relationship* first: reflect the feeling, affirm autonomy, apologize for any pressure. Don't defend yourself.

**Techniques for rolling with it:**
- **Reflect it (don't refute it):** Client: "I don't have time for this." → "Between work and the kids, finding an hour feels impossible right now." (She feels understood → tension drops → she often opens the door herself.)
- **Emphasize autonomy:** "You're right — it's completely your call how far you take this. I'm here either way." (Paradoxically lowers defenses.)
- **Reframe:** Client: "I've failed every diet." → "So you've actually experimented a lot and learned what *doesn't* work for you — that's useful information."
- **Shift focus / come alongside:** temporarily agree with the resistance to defuse it: "Maybe now isn't the right time to push hard on nutrition. What *does* feel doable?"

> **Copilot decision rule:** If the coach and client are in a tug-of-war, the coach is holding the rope. Tell the coach to **drop the rope**: reflect the client's position, affirm her autonomy, and stop pushing. Resistance is a signal to change the *coach's* approach, not to push harder.

## 5. Handling ambivalence — the normal state, not a problem

Ambivalence ("I want to change AND I want to stay the same") is the default human condition around change, not a red flag. The coach's job is to help her *resolve it in the direction of change* — by having her explore both sides but ending on the change side.

- **Don't take sides prematurely.** If the coach argues the "pro-change" side, the client is forced to voice the "anti-change" side (sustain talk). Let *her* build the pro-change case.
- **The decisional balance, used carefully:** explore the good things about the status quo AND the not-so-good things, then the benefits of change — but weight the conversation and the *closing summary* toward change talk. (Note: MI now cautions against a neutral decisional-balance when you have a clear health direction, because it can entrench ambivalence; use it mainly when the client is genuinely on the fence.)
- **Close by consolidating commitment:** after change talk emerges, ask a **key question** — "So where does this leave you?" or "What do you think you'll do?" — then help her make a small, concrete plan (link to implementation intentions, file 01).

## 6. Ready-to-use MI question scripts (copy/paste for the coach)

### Opening a check-in / building the agenda
- "What feels most important for us to focus on today?"
- "Where do you feel like things are going well, and where do you feel stuck?"

### Evoking change talk (the workhorses)
- "What made you decide to start this in the first place?"
- "What would you like to be different about your health / body / fitness?"
- "If you keep going exactly as you are now, where do you see yourself in a year?"
- "What worries you most about *not* changing anything?"
- "What are the best things that could happen if you made this change?"
- "How does getting fitter connect to what matters most to you — your family, your work, how you feel?"

### Scaling (importance & confidence)
- "On 0–10, how important is reaching this goal to you? … Why that number and not something lower?"
- "On 0–10, how confident are you that you can hit your sessions this week? … What would it take to move up one point?"

### When she's stuck / self-critical
- "You've done hard things before — tell me about a time you stuck to something that mattered."
- "That sounds really frustrating. What do you make of it?"
- "What's one small thing that *would* feel doable, even on a bad week?"

### When she's resistant / pushing back
- "It's totally your decision — I'm not here to push you. What feels right to *you*?"
- "Help me understand what's getting in the way — I want to build this around your real life."
- "You know yourself best. What do you think would actually work for you?"

### Closing / building commitment
- "So, given all that, what's the one thing you want to commit to before we talk next?"
- "When and where will that happen?" *(implementation intention)*
- "How confident are you, 0–10, that you'll do it?" *(if <7, shrink the commitment — file 01, §2)*

## 7. What MI is NOT (guardrails for the copilot)

- **Not persuasion in disguise.** If the coach is using "questions" to trap the client into an answer, it isn't MI and clients feel it.
- **Not endless reflection with no direction.** MI is *directive* — it steers toward a goal — just via evocation, not pressure.
- **Not therapy.** MI's warm, non-judgmental style is great for surfacing distress, but if what surfaces is clinical (depression, an eating disorder, trauma, self-harm), the coach's job is to hold space, not to counsel — and to **refer to a licensed professional** (file 05). MI's spirit makes that referral land gently.

> **Copilot summary heuristic:** For any "she's not doing it / she's pushing back / she's lost motivation" situation, default to: **fewer statements, more open questions and reflections; evoke *her* reasons; roll with resistance instead of fighting it; end on a small committed step.**
