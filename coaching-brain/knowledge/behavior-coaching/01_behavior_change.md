## 0. How the copilot should use this file

This is the reasoning engine behind every "what should I do with this client?" question. The core move is always the same: **diagnose which lever is missing (motivation, ability, prompt, or a psychological need), then pull the cheapest lever first.** Ability and environment are almost always cheaper to change than motivation. When a coach says "my client isn't doing the work," the answer is rarely "motivate them harder."

**LFG context baked in:** hybrid delivery (12-week app + WhatsApp programs fed by an in-person Dubai run club / bootcamp), premium price tier, largely-female professional GCC clientele, goals spanning fat loss, recomposition, strength, running (5k→half), HYROX, general fitness. Assume time-poor professionals, hot climate, Ramadan cycles, and privacy sensitivity throughout. *(Assumptions flagged where they drive advice.)*

## 1. The Fogg Behavior Model — the master diagnostic (B = MAP)

A behavior happens only when three things converge in the same moment: **Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt.** If any one is missing, the behavior doesn't fire. This is the single most useful lens for a coaching copilot because it turns "they're lazy / unmotivated" into a solvable triage.

- **Motivation** — how much the person wants to do it right now. The *least reliable* lever; it spikes and crashes daily (a hard workday, poor sleep, a bad weigh-in all tank it). Never build a plan that requires high motivation to run.
- **Ability** — how easy the behavior is *right now*, given time, money, physical effort, mental effort, and how much it disrupts their routine. The most reliable lever. Making a behavior easier moves more people than pumping motivation.
- **Prompt** — the cue that says "do it now." No prompt = no behavior, even with high motivation and ability. Most "forgot to do it" failures are missing prompts, not missing willpower.

**The action line (Fogg's curve):** for a given behavior, the harder it is (lower ability), the more motivation it needs to fire. So the design goal is to push behaviors *down and left* — make them so easy they fire even on a low-motivation day.

### Copilot triage: when a client isn't doing X, ask in this order
1. **Was there a prompt?** Did anything actually cue the behavior at the right moment? (Cheapest fix.)
2. **Was it easy enough?** Could they realistically do it given their day? If it needed 45 min they didn't have, that's an ability problem, not a discipline problem.
3. **Only then: motivation.** Was the behavior tied to something they actually want?

> **Decision rule:** Fix in order — Prompt → Ability → Motivation. Shrink the behavior before you try to inspire.

### Three prompt types (match the client's motivation level)
- **Spark** — a prompt that also boosts motivation (a before/after story, a reminder of their "why"). Use when ability is high but motivation is low.
- **Facilitator** — a prompt that makes the behavior easier (a pre-filled log, a 1-tap check-in). Use when motivation is high but ability is low ("I want to but it's hard").
- **Signal** — a plain reminder. Use only when both motivation and ability are already high; the person just needs the nudge.

## 2. Habit formation — Atomic Habits Four Laws + Tiny Habits

Habits are behaviors that have become automatic through a cue → routine → reward loop. To *build* a habit, make the loop obvious, attractive, easy, and satisfying. To *break* one, invert each law.

| Law (build) | What it means | LFG coaching move | Inversion (break a bad habit) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Make it obvious** | Design an unmissable cue | Anchor the workout to an existing routine ("after morning coffee"); lay out gym kit the night before | Make it invisible (remove the trigger — e.g. delete delivery apps) |
| **2. Make it attractive** | Bundle it with something wanted | Pair the run club with the social side; let her listen to a saved podcast only while training | Make it unattractive (highlight the cost) |
| **3. Make it easy** | Reduce friction; shrink the size | Two-Minute Rule: "just put on your shoes"; a 10-min minimum session on busy days | Make it hard (add friction) |
| **4. Make it satisfying** | Immediate reward / visible progress | Tick the habit in the app; a streak; a same-day WhatsApp "done ✅" | Make it unsatisfying (accountability, visible cost) |

### The two highest-leverage habit tools for a copilot to recommend

**A. Habit stacking (anchor a new behavior to an existing one).**
Formula: *"After I [existing habit], I will [new tiny habit]."* The existing routine is the reliable cue. Works because you're borrowing an automatic trigger instead of building one.
- "After I pour my morning coffee, I'll fill my 1L water bottle." (Hydration — critical in Dubai heat.)
- "After I put the kids to bed, I'll lay out my gym clothes."

**B. Implementation intentions (when/where/how plans).**
Formula: *"I will [behavior] at [time] in [place]."* Research consistently shows this closes the intention–action gap and accelerates habit formation; pairing it with mental imagery ("picture yourself finishing the session") strengthens the effect further.
- Weak: "I'll train more this week." → Strong: "I'll strength-train Mon/Wed/Fri at 6:30am at the DIFC gym."

### Start absurdly small (Tiny Habits + Two-Minute Rule)
New habits should be so small they feel almost too easy — "floss one tooth," "do one push-up," "put on shoes." The point isn't the one push-up; it's *establishing the automaticity and the identity of a person who shows up.* You scale up **after** the behavior is consistent, never before. Premium clients often want to start at 6 sessions/week; the copilot should flag this as the #1 predictor of early burnout and dropout.

> **Decision rule:** If adherence is below ~80% on a habit, the habit is too big or the prompt is missing. **Shrink it, don't scold.** Precision Nutrition's rule: pick ONE habit, confirm the client rates themselves ≥9/10 confident they'll do it daily; if under 9, make it smaller.

### Habit timelines — set honest expectations
"21 days to a habit" is a myth. Real-world estimates range widely (roughly a couple of months on average, longer for harder behaviors, and it varies enormously by person and habit). The copilot should coach the coach to sell *consistency over a season*, not a 3-week transformation — this directly protects retention in a 12-week program.

## 3. Motivation vs. discipline — reframe the whole debate

Clients (and coaches) treat motivation as the fuel. It isn't. Motivation is a **feeling that fluctuates**; you cannot bank it or summon it on demand. Betting adherence on motivation guarantees inconsistency.

What actually carries people through the flat, unmotivated middle of a 12-week block:
1. **Environment & systems** (Fogg + Clear) — reduce friction so the behavior fires on autopilot regardless of mood.
2. **Habits** — behaviors that no longer require a decision.
3. **Identity** — "I'm someone who trains" beats "I'm trying to lose weight." Identity-based habits are more durable because each rep is a vote for who they are. Coach the identity, not just the outcome.
4. **Autonomous motivation** (SDT, below) — the *quality* of motivation matters more than the quantity.

> **Copilot phrasing for the coach:** "Don't try to make her more motivated. Make the behavior smaller, the cue louder, and the environment easier — motivation will not show up reliably, so build a plan that runs without it."

## 4. Self-Determination Theory — the quality of motivation

SDT (Deci & Ryan) says humans have three basic psychological needs; satisfy them and motivation becomes *autonomous* (self-driven, durable). Frustrate them and motivation stays *controlled* (external pressure, guilt) — which predicts dropout. Systematic reviews in exercise consistently link autonomous motivation and coach autonomy-support to long-term adherence.

| Need | Definition | What frustrates it | LFG coaching moves to satisfy it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Acting from genuine choice, not pressure | Rigid, "do exactly this or else" plans; shame | Offer real choices ("A or B session today?"); ask her goals before prescribing; explain the *why* behind a plan; collaborate on the program |
| **Competence** | Feeling capable and improving | Programs too hard/complex; only tracking the scale (which stalls) | Set process goals she can win daily; track performance PRs (first unbroken 5k, +5kg deadlift); pitch difficulty just above current skill; celebrate small wins |
| **Relatedness** | Feeling connected and cared for | Transactional, faceless coaching; feeling like a number | The run club / bootcamp community; personal WhatsApp check-ins; remembering life details; a private female-friendly space |

**The autonomy-support principle is the highest-leverage coach behavior in the model:** coaches who provide choice, rationale, and empathy (rather than pressure and control) produce more autonomous motivation and better adherence. This is also why premium, high-touch coaching *should* out-retain cheap app subscriptions — if the coach actually uses the relationship.

> **Decision rule:** Every plan change should preserve at least one real choice for the client (autonomy), stay winnable (competence), and be delivered with warmth (relatedness). If a directive violates all three, expect resistance.

## 5. Stages of Change (Transtheoretical Model) — meet them where they are

People move through predictable stages. Matching your intervention to the stage is critical: pushing action-stage tactics (meal plans, programs) onto someone still ambivalent backfires and reads as pressure.

| Stage | Client mindset | **Wrong** move | **Right** move (copilot recommends) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Precontemplation** | Not considering change | Prescribe a program | Raise awareness gently; no pressure. (Rare in paying clients.) |
| **Contemplation** | "I should, but…" — ambivalent | Give a strict plan | Motivational Interviewing (see file 02): explore ambivalence, evoke *her* reasons |
| **Preparation** | Ready, planning to start soon | Overload with everything | Co-create a small concrete first step; set a start date |
| **Action** | Actively doing the work (<6 months) | Assume it's locked in | Reinforce habits, track wins, prevent early dropout (highest-risk window) |
| **Maintenance** | Sustaining (6+ months) | Go quiet | Guard against boredom & complacency; new goals (5k→10k, first HYROX) |
| **Relapse/Lapse** | Slipped back | Shame or "start over" | Normalize it, extract the lesson, re-enter at Preparation (see §8) |

Most paying LFG clients arrive in **Preparation or Action** but *emotionally* still carry Contemplation-stage ambivalence ("I want results but I'm scared / busy / skeptical"). The copilot should watch for that mismatch — it's the root of most early poor adherence.

## 6. Environment & context design — the cheapest lever

Behavior is a function of the person *and* their situation. Willpower is a weak, depletable resource; a well-designed environment does the work for free. This is Clear's "make it obvious/easy" and Fogg's ability lever applied to the client's actual life.

**Add friction to unwanted behaviors, remove it from wanted ones:**
- Wanted easier: pre-log meals in the app on Sunday; keep a packed gym bag in the car; schedule sessions as calendar blocks; put the run-club WhatsApp pinned.
- Unwanted harder: don't keep trigger foods at home; unfollow accounts that spike comparison (see file 04, body image).

**LFG-specific environment factors to design around:**
- **Hot-climate hydration & timing** — outdoor run club sessions early morning / evening; hydration as a stacked habit; heat is a real ability barrier May–Sept.
- **Ramadan** — training and nutrition windows shift entirely (post-iftar or pre-suhoor training, adjusted volume, hydration only in eating window). Redesign the environment/schedule for the month rather than expecting the normal plan to hold. (See file 04 and 01-adjacent programming notes.)
- **Professional schedules** — build around unpredictable work; a 10-minute "minimum viable session" beats an all-or-nothing 60-minute plan that gets skipped entirely.
- **Privacy/modesty** — home-workout options and female-only spaces lower the ability barrier for clients uncomfortable in mixed gyms.

## 7. Goal-setting — process goals over outcome goals

Outcome goals (lose 8kg, hit 75kg deadlift) are motivating to *set* but demotivating to *chase*, because the client doesn't control the outcome day-to-day (weight fluctuates, progress stalls). Process/behavior goals are fully in the client's control, which protects competence and adherence.

- **Outcome goal:** the destination — useful for direction, dangerous as the daily scoreboard. Keep one, revisit monthly.
- **Process goal:** the behaviors that get there — the daily scoreboard. "3 strength sessions + 8k steps/day + protein at breakfast this week."

**Make goals SMART-ish but behavior-first,** and pair each with an implementation intention (§2). The copilot should translate every outcome goal a coach reports into 1–3 controllable process goals:
- "Client wants to lose 10kg" → process: "3 sessions/week, 7k+ steps daily, protein target 4/7 days to start."
- "Client wants to run a half-marathon" → process: "long run every Saturday, 2 easy runs midweek, follow the progression."

> **Decision rule:** If a client is anxious or discouraged, they are over-focused on an outcome they don't control. Redirect attention to the *process goal they hit this week.* Win the week, not the scale.

## 8. Managing lapses — the make-or-break skill

A lapse (one missed workout, one off-plan meal) is normal and inevitable. The danger is the **abstinence-violation effect**: the client interprets one slip as total failure ("I've ruined it, might as well quit"), and a single lapse cascades into a full relapse and dropout. Given that >50% of exercisers quit within six months and loss of motivation drives ~38% of cancellations, lapse-recovery is a retention system, not a soft skill.

**The copilot's lapse-response protocol (coach it to the coach):**
1. **Normalize immediately** — "Missing a session is part of the process, not the end of it." Remove the moral charge.
2. **Never let a slip become a spiral** — the rule is "**never miss twice.**" One skipped workout is an event; two in a row starts a new (bad) habit.
3. **Shrink the re-entry** — after a lapse, don't demand the full plan back. Prescribe a tiny win to rebuild momentum (a 15-min session, one on-plan meal).
4. **Investigate the cause without judgment** — was it prompt, ability, or motivation (§1)? Redesign that lever. A lapse is data.
5. **Reaffirm identity** — "You're still someone who trains — you just had a normal week." Protect the self-image that drives the behavior.

> **Copilot next-best-move template:** "Client missed 3 days → (1) message to normalize, no guilt; (2) offer a 15-min re-entry session today; (3) ask one diagnostic question about what got in the way; (4) fix that specific lever for next week. Do NOT reprimand or pile on 'make-up' work."

## 9. Quick-reference decision tree (copilot cheat sheet)

```
Client not doing the work?
 ├─ No cue fired?        → add a prompt (habit stack / calendar block / WhatsApp nudge)
 ├─ Too hard / no time?  → shrink it (Two-Minute Rule, 10-min minimum, remove friction)
 ├─ Doesn't want to?     → check SDT need: choice? winnable? cared-for? + MI (file 02)
 └─ Slipped after doing well? → lapse protocol (§8): normalize → tiny re-entry → fix the lever

Client wants a big outcome fast?
 → keep the outcome goal for direction, coach the process goals for the daily scoreboard,
   set honest habit timelines, start smaller than they want.
```
