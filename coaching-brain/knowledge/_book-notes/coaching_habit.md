# The Coaching Habit — Distilled for a Fitness Coaching Brain

> Source: Bungay Stanier, *The Coaching Habit: Say Less, Ask More* (Box of Crayons Press, 2016). Original paraphrase and adaptation for fitness coaching. No verbatim text reproduced.

---

## The one idea to internalize

Give a little less advice, show a little more curiosity. That single behavior shift is the whole book. Most coaches default to advice-giving because certainty feels productive and ambiguity feels uncomfortable, but the first thing a client tells you is rarely the real issue, and the answer you hand them lands weaker than the answer they reach themselves. Curiosity, held for a few seconds longer than feels natural, does more than a good tip.

For a fitness coach this matters because your whole business runs on adherence, and adherence runs on ownership. A client who decides their own next step is far more likely to do it than one who was told. Bungay Stanier frames the goal as building a *habit* of asking, not memorizing scripts, so that curiosity survives a rushed check-in when you are tired and behind.

---

## Why "say less, ask more" beats advice-giving

Bungay Stanier gives three practical reasons advice underperforms:

1. **You solve the wrong problem.** The presenting complaint ("I can't lose weight") is usually a symptom or a stand-in for the real challenge ("I keep quitting after week three"). Jump to a solution and you fix the wrong thing, expertly.
2. **You do work that isn't yours.** Every time you supply the answer, the client learns to bring you the problem instead of thinking. You become the bottleneck; they stay dependent.
3. **Advice doesn't stick.** People create durable memory by generating an answer themselves, not by receiving one. Told information mostly evaporates; a conclusion the client reaches gets encoded.

He also names a trap he calls the **Advice Monster**: the reflex, fired in seconds, to leap in with your fix before you understand the situation. He cites a study where doctors interrupted patients after roughly eighteen seconds. Coaches do the same. Taming the monster is not "never give advice", it is delaying it until the client has offered their own thinking first.

**Taming the Advice Monster in practice (fitness):** when a client asks a direct "what should I do about X?", don't answer yet. Say: *"Good question. I've got a couple of ideas I'll share. But first, what are your initial thoughts?"* Then "And what else could you try?" Add your idea only after theirs is on the table. This keeps ownership with them and often makes your eventual input sharper because you now know the real constraint.

## The Seven Essential Questions

Each is short enough to ask in under a minute. Ask them close to verbatim; the wording is load-bearing.

### 1. The Kickstart — "What's on your mind?"
Opens a real conversation fast. It is deliberately in the middle: open enough that the client picks what matters most, focused enough that they go to the live issue, not to trivia. It dissolves three time-wasters: small talk, the same-old standing agenda, and the "default diagnosis" where you both assume you already know the topic.

Follow it with the **3P focusing frame** to decide what layer to work on:
- **Project** — the content itself (the program, the macros, the schedule).
- **People** — a relationship (their partner who cooks, a training buddy, you).
- **Pattern** — their own recurring behavior (all-or-nothing, weekend blowouts, quitting at week three). This is where the deepest, most durable change happens.

### 2. The AWE Question — "And what else?"
The single most useful question in the book. Three words that surface more options, keep you from jumping in, and buy you a moment to think. The first answer is almost never the only or best one. Ask it three to five times, no more. When the client says "nothing else," that is success, not failure; it means the vein is mined. Then move on.

### 3. The Focus Question — "What's the real challenge here for you?"
Stops you solving the first problem instead of the real one. Every word earns its place: *"real"* implies there are several and forces a choice; *"for you"* pins it to this person instead of an abstract lecture. Use it against three fog-makers: too many problems at once (ask "if you had to pick one, which is the real challenge?"), venting about a third party ("I get what's going on with them, what's the real challenge here *for you*?"), and vague big-picture talk ("I have a sense of the overall thing, what's the real challenge here for you?").

### 4. The Foundation Question — "What do you want?"
The hardest question to answer honestly, and the heart of an adult-to-adult relationship: being able to ask for what you want knowing the answer may be no. Clients often don't know, can't voice it, or hint instead of stating it. Listen underneath the stated *want* for the deeper *need* (Bungay Stanier borrows nine: affection, creation, recreation, freedom, identity, understanding, participation, protection, subsistence). "I want a meal plan" might really be a need for *understanding* or *freedom from deciding*. For bonus power, answer it yourself too so the exchange is mutual.

A softer sibling from solution-focused therapy is the **miracle question**: *"If you woke up tomorrow and this was solved, what would be different? How would you know?"* It aims people at a real outcome instead of a 10% tweak.

### 5. The Lazy Question — "How can I help?"
Two payoffs: it forces the client to make a clear request, and it stops you from Rescuer mode, leaping in to fix what they never asked you to fix. Blunter version: *"What do you want from me?"* Soften any question with *"Out of curiosity…"* or *"Just so I'm clear…"*. You are never obligated to say yes; you can say no, or "not that but this instead", or "let me think about it." Ties to the **Karpman Drama Triangle** (Victim / Persecutor / Rescuer). Coaches live in Rescuer: over-helping creates dependent clients, burns you out, and reduces impact. "How can I help?" pulls you out.

### 6. The Strategic Question — "If you're saying yes to this, what are you saying no to?"
Strategy is choosing what *not* to do. Adding a new commitment (a 5am gym slot, a strict prep) is empty unless something makes room for it. It surfaces two no's: the no of omission (what this yes automatically rules out) and the no of commission (what you must actively drop to make the yes real). Antidote to the "planning fallacy" (we always underestimate what things cost) and loss-aversion (we cling to what we already do). Related discipline: **say yes more slowly** — stay curious before committing rather than reflexively agreeing.

### 7. The Learning Question — "What was most useful for you?"
Ends every conversation. People don't learn from being told, or even from doing; they learn when they *recall and reflect*. This question assumes the talk was useful, forces them to name the one big takeaway (not twelve), makes it personal with *"for you"*, gives you feedback on what to do more of, and by finishing on a high note makes the whole session remembered more favorably (the peak-end rule). Never ask "was this useful?" (a yes/no that skips the insight).

**The Coaching Bookends:** open with #1 ("What's on your mind?"), close with #7 ("What was most useful for you?"). Start fast, finish strong.

## The 1-2-3 combo (a ready conversation spine)

1. **Kickstart:** "What's on your mind?"
2. **Check for more:** "And what else is going on?"
3. **Focus:** "So what's the real challenge here *for you*?" (then AWE it: "And what else is a real challenge here?")

This alone handles most check-ins.

## How to build the coaching-question habit

Behavior is ~45% habit, so intention isn't enough. Bungay Stanier's **New Habit Formula** has three parts:

- **When this happens…** — name the exact trigger (location, time, emotion, a person, or the preceding action). Specific beats vague: not "at check-ins" but "when a client opens with 'I've been bad this week.'"
- **Instead of…** — name the old habit you're stopping (usually: launching into advice / prescribing before you understand).
- **I will…** — the new sixty-second behavior (ask one of the seven questions).

Plus: attach it to a **reason bigger than you** (better results and less burnout for your clients), practice it in small deep-practice reps, celebrate small wins, and expect to slip and simply get back on. Master one question first, get it in your bones, then add another. Start with an easy client. Buddy up for accountability.

## Adapting the seven questions for a fitness coach

### Check-ins (weekly / progress reviews)
Ready-to-use openers and probes:
- "What's on your mind about this week?" *(Kickstart)*
- "Walk me through the week — what actually happened?" then "And what else?" *(AWE, to get past the polished first summary)*
- "Of everything you just listed, what's the real challenge for you right now?" *(Focus)*
- "What do you want this next block to actually look like?" *(Foundation)*
- "How can I make this next week easier for you?" *(Lazy)*
- "If we go all-in on the 5-day plan, what are you going to drop to make room?" *(Strategic)*
- "What was most useful for you in this check-in?" *(Learning — always close here)*

### Consultations / discovery calls
- "What's on your mind that made you reach out now?" *(Kickstart — surfaces the real trigger, not the generic goal)*
- "You said you want to lose weight — and what else are you really after?" *(AWE → find the deeper need: confidence, energy, keeping up with kids)*
- "What's the real challenge that's stopped this from working before?" *(Focus — the honest answer is your whole coaching plan)*
- "Six months from now, what has to be true for you to say this was worth it?" *(miracle / Foundation)*
- "How can I best support you between sessions?" *(Lazy — sets the working contract)*

### Hard conversations (missed sessions, stalled progress, thinking of quitting, awkward money/renewal talk)
Lead with curiosity, not correction — it lowers the client's threat response and keeps them engaged:
- Instead of "you have to be more consistent": *"What's the real challenge here for you with staying consistent?"* *(Focus)*
- When they vent about their schedule/spouse/work: *"I hear all that. What's the real challenge in it for you?"* *(pulls it back from the ghost to them)*
- When they're overwhelmed and adding more won't help: *"If we're serious about this goal, what do we need to take off your plate?"* *(Strategic)*
- When you feel yourself about to rescue (redo their plan, chase them, over-give): pause and ask *"How can I help?"* or *"What do you want from me here?"* — then decide if you'll say yes.
- To de-escalate a tense exchange, soften with *"Out of curiosity, what were you hoping would be different?"*
- Close even a hard talk on the Learning Question: *"What was most useful about us talking this through?"* — it repairs the relationship and makes the session remembered better.

## Coaching cautions from the book
- Not every moment needs a question. If a client asks where the folder is, tell them. Questions are for slowing the rush to *default* advice, not for being annoying.
- Don't over-generate options; three to five is plenty (too many causes paralysis).
- There is still a place for your expertise. The order matters: their thinking first, your advice second.
- Find your own voice with these questions rather than reciting them mechanically. The habit of curiosity is the point.
