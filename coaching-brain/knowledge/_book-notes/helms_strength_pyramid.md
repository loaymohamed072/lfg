# The Muscle & Strength Training Pyramid — Coaching Distillation

**Source:** Eric Helms, *The Muscle and Strength Training Pyramid, v2.0 — Training* (2nd ed., 2019). Original paraphrase for coaching use; no verbatim text reproduced. Written with Dr. Michael Zourdos' RPE/RIR research lab.

This is the applied core a coach can program from. The pyramid is a **priority map**: get the bottom levels right and they carry ~80% of the result. Numbers are starting ranges you autoregulate off the athlete's response, not rules. Helms' framing: "put first that which is most important," then learn how to adjust each variable.

---

## The pyramid, in priority order

Six levels, foundation to peak, with **periodization as the overarching theme threading all of them**:

1. **Adherence** (foundation)
2. **Volume, Intensity, Frequency** (VIF)
3. **Progression** (houses progression models, periodization, and deloads)
4. **Exercise Selection**
5. **Rest Periods**
6. **Lifting Tempo**

**Why the order matters:** the levels sit in descending order of impact on results. A program you can't stick to returns nothing, so adherence is the base. Given adherence, the training stimulus itself is governed by how much (volume), how hard (intensity), and how often (frequency) — VIF is where most of the adaptation is bought. Progression turns a good session into long-term gains. Only after those are handled do exercise selection, rest, and tempo add their smaller finishing touches. Helms estimates the **bottom three-to-four levels produce ~80% of progress**; the top levels mostly just keep you from doing something wrong. He is confident the *order* won't change (it rests on fundamental principles) even as the *recommendations inside each level* update with new research. Coaching implication: never let a client obsess over tempo or rest timing while their volume, intensity, or adherence is a mess.

---

## Level 1 — Adherence

A program only works if it gets done. Three conditions for a sustainable plan:
- **Realistic** — the schedule (time frame and weekly slots) has to fit the client's actual life.
- **Enjoyable** — they have to be able to train this way for a long stretch.
- **Flexible** — built-in room to absorb the unexpected. Two forms of flexibility he stresses: adjusting **when stress is high** (life doesn't stop for lifting), and adjusting **based on daily energy/recovery** (autoregulation). Match hard sessions to recovered days and easier sessions to depleted days — shown to help both strength and adherence.

## Level 2 — Volume, Intensity, Frequency (VIF)

**Starting recommendations:**
- **Volume: 10–20 sets per muscle group / movement pattern per week.** Counted as sets (v2 dropped the old "reps per body part" metric). Volume shares a **dose-response with hypertrophy up to a point** — more is better until it isn't, then it's counterproductive.
- **Intensity** (guided by rep range + proximity to failure, not %1RM):
  - *Strength:* ⅔–¾ of volume in the **1–6 rep** range, remainder in **6–15**, all at **RPE 5–10**.
  - *Hypertrophy:* ⅔–¾ of volume in the **6–12 rep** range, remainder in **1–6 and 12–20**, at **RPE 5–10**.
- **Frequency: 2+ times per muscle group / movement pattern per week.** Frequency is a tool to spread volume so sessions stay high-quality, plus more practice reps for skill and better recovery distribution.

**Volume/frequency by training age** (guideline for placing yourself inside the 10–20 range):
| Training age | Weekly sets / muscle-movement | Weekly frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Novice | 10–12 | 2–3 |
| Intermediate | 13–15 | 3–4 |
| Advanced | 16–20 | 3–5 |

Higher volume → spread across more sessions to protect quality. Two days/week works for novices but becomes infeasible later because the requisite volume makes sessions too long. **Fitness-Fatigue model** underpins all of this: training builds both fitness (gradual, lasting) and fatigue (proportional to workload; masks fitness). You program to accumulate fitness while managing fatigue.

## Level 3 — Progression (+ periodization + deloads)

**Progressive overload, but only as needed.** Volume should rise over a training career, but increase it only when progress stalls, not reflexively.

**Progression models by training age:**
- **Novice — linear/session-to-session:** add **5 lb** to the bar each session on compounds (**10 lb** on squat/deadlift), leaving a little in the tank for form. Micro-plates extend this when 5 lb jumps stop.
- **Intermediate — linear (weekly) + double progression:** ~**5 lb every ~5 weeks** on compounds is realistic long-term. For isolation work, use **double progression** (add reps within a range, then add load) since you can't keep adding load. Wave loading also fits here.
- **Advanced — periodized blocks:** progress measured over months. **Block periodization**: Accumulation (~6 wk, build work capacity/volume), Intensification (~4 wk, drop volume, raise intensity), Realization/taper (~2 wk, peak and test). A full cycle can run up to ~4 months, so advanced lifters may only test strength ~3x/year. Also uses **DUP (daily undulating periodization)** — rotating rep/intensity focus across the week (e.g. hypertrophy day 6–12 @ RPE 5–8, power day 1–3 reps @ 75–90% 1RM / RPE 5–7, low volume).

**RPE / RIR autoregulation** (the intensity-of-effort tool, from Tuchscherer, researched by Helms/Zourdos):
- RPE is anchored to **reps in reserve**: **10 = failure, 9 = 1 RIR, 8 = 2 RIR**, etc.
- Experienced lifters estimate RIR accurately; beginners often can't. Calibrate it: film sets and rate on replay; run a short 3–6 week block taking sets to true failure (with a spotter) to learn your ceiling; have a partner or coach rate your videos; or do a set to a target RPE then push to failure to check accuracy.
- Use RPE to autoregulate load day-to-day so effort stays in the intended zone regardless of daily readiness.

**Deloads / fatigue management** — built *into* the plan, not bolted on when broken:
- Fatigue-Fitness logic: overload drives adaptation in trained lifters but also builds residual fatigue that suppresses performance. Planned low-stress periods let fatigue dissipate so fitness re-expresses (functional **overreaching** → supercompensation).
- **Novices:** deload = simply **drop the load ~10%** next time the exercise recurs (no complexity needed).
- **Beyond novice:** use a proper **taper — reduce volume, keep intensity roughly the same.**
- v2 adds a **"reactive" deload** model: deload in response to accumulated fatigue/stalled progress, not only on a fixed calendar.

## Level 4 — Exercise Selection

Governed by **specificity** and **efficiency**:
- **Specificity:** you gain most at what you practice. Unfamiliar movements are less effective for hypertrophy until you learn them; a *degree* of variety still has value.
- **Efficiency:** compounds for the biggest strength/mass return; isolations to bring up **weak points** (a lagging muscle for hypertrophy, or a **sticking region** in a lift for strength). Address weak points broadly.
- Also covers exercise order, range of motion (favor full ROM), and technique. Exercise selection can be **autoregulated** too (swap based on how a movement feels day-to-day).

## Level 5 — Rest Periods

The "short rest for hypertrophy via hormones/metabolic stress" rationale is weak — **no study has shown greater hypertrophy from rest intervals between 1 and 5 minutes**. Recommendation:
- **Rest until you can perform your best on the next set.**
- If you tend to rush: clock it — **≥1.5 min** for smaller muscle groups, **≥2.5 min** between compound lifts (straight sets).
- Time-savers when needed: **antagonist paired sets** (~2 min for push/pull compounds, ~1 min for isolations), plus drop sets and rest-pause sets (track them, compare only to like sets). Reduced rest has a place for efficiency, not as a growth trigger.

## Level 6 — Lifting Tempo

The smallest lever. "Time under tension" as a growth driver is overstated — load threshold matters more than deliberately slowing reps. Recommendation:
- **Control the eccentric** to a reasonable degree (position control matters, e.g. lowering a squat/bench under control), but **don't purposefully grind reps slow** for hypertrophy.
- **Lift the concentric with maximal intent / force** (explosive intent), even with lighter loads.
- That's it — controlled lowering + forceful raising, and you've cleared the pyramid.
