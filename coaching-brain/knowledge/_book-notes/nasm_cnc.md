# NASM Certified Nutrition Coach — Coaching Distillation

**Source:** NASM (National Academy of Sports Medicine), *Certified Nutrition Coach* course textbook. Original paraphrase for coaching use; no verbatim text reproduced. Numbers are the book's stated ranges and starting points, not laws. Program by principle, adjust off the client's own data.

What makes the NASM approach distinctive: it is **scope-first and behavior-first**. NASM trains a coach to *educate and empower clients to make their own decisions*, not to prescribe. It leans hard on client-centered coaching (motivational interviewing + stages of change) and treats **adherence, not diet type, as the master variable**. The nutrition science is mainstream and evidence-graded; the differentiator is the coaching model wrapped around it and the strict line between a coach and a Registered Dietitian.

---

## 1. Scope of practice — the boundary NASM enforces hardest

A Nutrition Coach is a **certified** professional, not a **licensed** one. The licensed clinical role is the Registered Dietitian Nutritionist (RDN/RD), a legally protected title (degree + ACEND coursework + supervised hours + CDR national exam). Only RDNs and other licensed providers (MD, ARNP, PA, PharmD, CDE) may prescribe clinical/therapeutic diets. Rules vary by country and US state; the coach is responsible for checking local law.

**A Nutrition Coach CAN:** evaluate current eating and give general guidance; teach balanced macronutrient intake; promote public guidelines (USDA MyPlate, UK Eatwell); recommend gradual, consistent dietary changes; give general guidance to healthy populations and typical recreational athletes (a 10K, masters swimming); offer eating behaviors known to reduce disease risk; provide accountability, motivation, and behavior-change tactics; refer.

**A Nutrition Coach CANNOT:** write specific meal plans or daily menus; prescribe specific supplements or vitamin/mineral dosages; prescribe severe caloric restriction; promote fasting, detox, or extreme practices; prescribe elimination of food groups; prescribe diets for chronic disease (diabetes, heart disease); provide nutrition therapy to treat/prevent disease; contradict a licensed provider; write exercise programming unless separately certified (e.g., NASM-CPT).

**Refer out (stop or pause sessions) when:** no progress toward goals; decline in athletic performance; any change in health status (chest pain, blood-glucose trouble, chronic pain/fatigue, GI changes); pregnancy or lactation (refer to OB); extreme preoccupation with weight or signs of disordered eating; signs of depression/anxiety/addiction; suicidal ideation (call emergency services immediately). Clients with a chronic condition wanting extreme sport go to an RDN/CSSD/specialist.

**SCOFF screen** (informal, coach may work into conversation, never diagnose): **S**ick (make yourself vomit when full), **C**ontrol (lost control of eating), **O**ne stone (>14 lb / ~6.4 kg lost in 3 months), **F**at (believe you're fat when others say thin), **F**ood (food dominates life). **2+ "yes" answers = refer** (likely anorexia/bulimia).

Client confidentiality: no personal client discussion without written consent; before/after photos and metrics need written consent.

## 2. The coaching process — Assess → Educate → Implement

- **Assess:** health-history questionnaire, verbal interview, and (if appropriate) physical/body-comp assessment. Cover goals, stumbling blocks, food preferences/aversions/allergies, hydration, food triggers, exercise and daily activity. Physical: height, weight, body composition, girths (arm, neck, chest, waist, hip, thigh, calf). Follow with a **3-to-5-day food journal** (self-monitoring correlates strongly with weight loss; journalers lose more than non-journalers). This screening also flags anyone out of scope.
- **Educate** using adult-learning principles: information must be **relevant, built on prior experience, practical, and perceived as important** to *this* client. Teach the "why." Help the client sort real evidence from media noise.
- **Implement:** co-build an action plan that fits the client's schedule and budget (weekday vs weekend, summer vs holiday plans). Pair with resistance training via a CPT where relevant, because **up to ~25% of each pound lost can come from lean tissue** without it.

## 3. Behavior change — Transtheoretical / Stages of Change

Change is a **process, not an event**. Six stages (Prochaska/DiClemente): **precontemplation → contemplation → preparation → action → maintenance → termination**, with relapse expected as part of the loop. **Match your intervention to the stage.** Making plans too early (before the client is ready) breeds resistance.

| Stage | Client state | Coach's move |
|---|---|---|
| Precontemplation | Unaware/unwilling | Empathetic + reflective listening, value exploration, build rapport, avoid argument, one small change + affirmation, build self-efficacy |
| Contemplation | Ambivalent, weighing it | Resolve ambivalence, surface personally-relevant biomarkers (bloodwork, BP), decisional-balance sheet, affirmations |
| Preparation | Intends to act | Assess commitment strength, co-build a feasible plan with when/where/how/with-whom |
| Action | Doing it | Revise plans, foster self-efficacy, harness social support, use implementation intentions |
| Maintenance | Sustaining | Restructure environment to remove temptation, identify relapses early, forgive slip-ups, keep social support |

Four precontemplator sub-types and the move for each: **Reluctant** (lacks knowledge → sensitive feedback), **Rebellious** (adamant → reassure no one will force them, offer a menu of options), **Resigned** (feels hopeless → instill hope, explore barriers), **Rationalizing** (feels no personal risk → empathy, let them voice the pros of their behavior).

**Implementation intentions** = if-then plans that hand behavior control to the environment: "If my coffee is brewing, I eat an apple." **Motivation** is intrinsic (enjoys the process, sticks longer) and extrinsic (rewards/recognition). Both show up; steer toward intrinsic. **Habits** are the real leverage point.

## 4. Motivational Interviewing — the NASM coaching engine

MI is a **way of being with a client**: collaboration, drawing out the client's own motivation, and preserving autonomy. There is no script. Do not create motivation, evoke it.

**Five strategies of MI:**
1. **Express empathy** through reflective listening (unconditional positive regard: assume the client is trying their best; avoid the *fundamental attribution error* of blaming character).
2. **Develop discrepancy** between the client's values/goals and current behavior (the "ideal comparator" vs current self).
3. **Support self-efficacy.**
4. **Avoid arguments** (a coach never wins by arguing).
5. **Roll with resistance** (flow with sustain talk, don't oppose it).

**Core communication skills** (NASM's OARS-style toolkit): **open-ended questions** (start with how/what), **affirmations** (name *why* they succeeded, e.g. "you prioritized it"; avoid starting with "I"), **reflective listening** ("It sounds like…", "You feel like…", "Tell me more…"), and **summarizing**. Rapport, credibility, and trust are direct drivers of adherence.

**Change talk vs sustain talk:** change talk = client voicing benefits of changing / costs of staying the same ("I'm sick of feeling low-energy"). It appears when there's a gap between behavior and values. Listen for it, evoke more of it, and reflect it back.

**Values work is the NASM signature move.** Goals are end-states that express durable **values**; values outlive goals and keep guiding behavior after a goal is hit. **Value exploration** = help the client discover *why* the goal matters, then build behavior-oriented goals from it. A goal rooted only in an external outcome (a number) tends to collapse once hit; a value-rooted goal sustains maintenance.

**Self-Determination Theory** underpins it: three needs = **autonomy, competence, relatedness**. Autonomy support ("in the end it's your choice, what would you want to try?") produces longer adherence and fewer relapses than a directive coach. **Self-efficacy** (Bandura) is built four ways: performance accomplishments (mastery of an attainable step), vicarious experience (other clients' stories), verbal persuasion (remind them of past wins), and managing physiological states (reduce anxiety). When confidence is low, **shrink the task** until it's winnable. Scaling tool: "How confident, 0-10?" then "Why a 3 and not a 1?" to evoke the client's own confidence talk.

## 5. Goal setting

- **Only ~8% of New Year's resolutions succeed.** Setting a goal doesn't change behavior; setting the *right kind* does.
- **Outcome goals** (lose 20 lb) are partly out of the client's control; useful as a destination but should **not** be the focus.
- **Performance goals** (personal standard, e.g. hit a protein target) and especially **process goals** (the specific behaviors: prep lunch 5 days, one veg per meal) are what drive results. Anchor coaching on process.
- Goals should be specific and measurable, realistic in commitment/difficulty, and carry a concrete strategy. Subjective goals can work if paired with specific process behaviors.

## 6. Energy balance and metabolism

Governed by the **First Law of Thermodynamics** — all calories are accounted for (used, stored, or lost as heat). Body-mass change follows energy in vs energy out, but both sides are more complex than a spreadsheet.

**Energy Out has four parts:**
- **RMR (Resting Metabolic Rate)** — the majority of daily burn; proportional to lean mass; falls ~2%/decade (men) and ~3%/decade (women) mostly from lean-mass loss; drops ~0.01 kcal/min per 1% rise in body fatness.
- **TEF (Thermic Effect of Food)** — ~**10%** of daily expenditure; **protein has the highest TEF** of the macros.
- **TEA (Thermic Effect of Activity)** — most variable component, ~**15-30%** of daily expenditure.
- **NEAT (Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis)** — everything that isn't sleeping/eating/formal exercise (walking, fidgeting, chores). Can add up to **~2,000 kcal/day** in an active person. Programming NEAT (steps, standing, walking meetings, stairs) is one of the most practical levers for a client's deficit.

Average TDEE baseline ~2,000 kcal (varies widely). **Sleep matters to energy balance:** sleeping ≤5 h/night links to ~3.7x higher obesity odds in men, ~2.3x in women vs 7-8 h; short sleep drove ~**385 extra kcal/day** in one review, plus lower leptin, higher ghrelin, higher cortisol.

**Hormones:** thyroid hormone sets metabolic rate (hypo → weight gain, lower burn); cortisol (stress) raises blood glucose; testosterone influences body-fat/muscle. Appetite hormones: **leptin** (from fat cells, suppresses appetite; obesity can bring leptin resistance), **ghrelin** ("hunger hormone" from the stomach), CCK, NPY, insulin, glucagon.

Coaching translation: energy balance is real, but you manipulate it through **satiety, NEAT, protein, sleep, and adherence**, not by chasing perfect calorie math.

## 7. Macronutrients — the numbers

**Protein** (4 kcal/g):
- RDA is a floor: **0.8 g/kg** (46 g women, 56 g men) — set to prevent deficiency, not to optimize.
- NASM's practical daily range: **1.2-2.2 g/kg**. General population **1.2-1.6 g/kg (0.54-0.7 g/lb)**; athletes / maximizing muscle **1.6-2.2 g/kg (0.7-1.0 g/lb)**. Preserving muscle in a deficit can justify **2.3-3.1 g/kg**, but that doesn't build more muscle than 1.6-2.2.
- Older adults (65+): **0.9-1.6 g/kg** (higher than RDA due to anabolic resistance).
- Very high intakes (3.0-4.4 g/kg) show no adverse body-composition effects. When in doubt, err toward more protein, not less.
- Distribution: **~0.4-0.55 g/kg across ~4 servings** maximizes muscle protein synthesis; practically **20-40 g protein per meal**. If total daily protein is met and muscle isn't the goal, timing barely matters.
- **Leucine** is the MPS trigger: optimal ~**0.05 g/kg**, signals for ~3 h. Absorption ~85% plant / ~95% animal protein.

**Carbohydrates** (4 kcal/g):
- Minimum **130 g/day** (brain/CNS glucose). AMDR **45-65% of calories**. **Added sugar <10%** of calories (WHO ideal <5%); ~6 tsp/100 kcal for women.
- **Fiber:** women **25 g/day**, men **38 g/day** (or **14 g per 1,000 kcal**). Most people fall short. Soluble (oats, beans, fruit) vs insoluble (structural plant fiber).
- Athlete carb needs scale with training volume: **3-5 g/kg/day** (general/low-volume/skill), **5-8 g/kg** (moderate-volume high-intensity, 2-3 h/day), **8-10 g/kg** (high-volume endurance, 3-6 h/day), **~12 g/kg** (>6 h/day). Glycogen storage ~2,000-2,500 kcal (up to 3,000+ in endurance athletes).

**Fats** (9 kcal/g):
- AMDR **20-35% of calories** (flexible; athletes lower to make room for carbs, general-pop can go higher, even ~50% if fiber/micros/carbs are adequate).
- **Saturated fat <10%** of calories; replace with unsaturated. Avoid trans fat.
- **Essential fatty acids:** omega-3 (ALA, and the more active EPA/DHA from fish) and omega-6 (linoleic). Omega-3s support cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory outcomes.

**Alcohol** (7.1 kcal/g, "empty" calories): moderate = **≤1 drink/day (women), ≤2/day (men)**. High-risk = **4+/day or 8+/week (women), 5+/day or 15+/week (men)**.

## 8. Hydration

- Involuntary/voluntary dehydration is common: **thirst lags sweat loss**. Sweat rates can hit **up to 3 L/hour** in heat. Don't let losses exceed **~2% body mass** (performance drops) and never approach ~8% (danger). Over-drinking can cause exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH) — a real risk, so more is not always better.
- Monitoring: **urine color** and **body-weight change** pre/post exercise (each lost kg ≈ ~1 L to replace). Aim to replace ~**125-150% of sweat losses** over recovery when large volumes are lost.
- During prolonged exercise: **~0.4-0.8 L fluid/hour**; add carbohydrate + electrolytes (sodium, potassium) for long/heavy-sweat sessions. **Milk** outperformed sports drinks and water for rehydration retention in comparison studies.

---

## 9. Micronutrients and supplements

**Micronutrients:** whole-food-first. Commonly under-consumed in typical Western diets: **vitamin D, calcium, potassium, iron, fiber, magnesium** (also B12 for older adults/vegans; iodine; vitamin E). Water-soluble (B-complex, C) aren't stored and excess is excreted; fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) store in tissue (over-supplementation risk). A daily **multivitamin/mineral** is a reasonable insurance gap-filler (a coach educates, but per scope cannot *prescribe* dosages).

**Supplements — grade by evidence (IOC hierarchy; anecdote/testimonial is the weakest tier).** Strong-evidence performance/muscle group: **protein, creatine monohydrate, essential amino acids, caffeine, beta-alanine, sodium bicarbonate, dietary nitrate**.
- **Creatine monohydrate** (>1,000 studies): load **20-25 g/day (or ~0.3 g/kg) split over 5-7 days**, then maintain **3-10 g/day**; or skip loading and take **3-5 g/day for ~4 weeks** (slower). Serving-size labels often understate the effective dose.
- **Caffeine:** ergogenic at **3-6 mg/kg** (raises EE, may blunt appetite); keep total **≤400-500 mg/day**; higher doses/stacking cause jitteriness, insomnia, GI upset.
- **HMB:** **1.5-3 g/day**, benefit mainly in untrained/older individuals via reduced muscle damage; weak effect on trained lifters.
- Supplement industry is loosely regulated; verify ingredient evidence and that the product's form/dose matches the research. Coaches recommend clients discuss specific supplements with their RDN/physician/pharmacist.

## 10. Nutrient timing

- **Anabolic window** is wider than the old "30-45 min" claim — think **1-2 hours** and, more importantly, **total daily protein and carbohydrate dominate over precise timing** for most clients.
- Pre-exercise: meals/snacks **1-2 h before** so food digests.
- During endurance (>60 min): **30-60 g carbohydrate/hour**, plus **0.4-0.8 L fluid/hour**.
- Post-exercise (matters most for rapid glycogen turnaround / two-a-days): **carbs 1-1.2 g/kg/hour for 4-6 h**; **protein 20-30 g (0.25-0.3 g/kg)** soon after. For a general client training once a day, hitting daily totals is what counts.

## 11. Programming — putting it together (the NASM calc sequence)

**Step 1 — BMR.** Default **Mifflin–St. Jeor** (kg, cm, years):
- Men: `9.99×wt + 6.25×ht − 4.92×age + 5`
- Women: `9.99×wt + 6.25×ht − 4.92×age − 161`
- If body-fat % is known, **Katch–McArdle** `370 + 21.6×LBM` or **Cunningham** `500 + 22×LBM` (LBM in kg) are lean-mass-based alternatives.

**Step 2 — TDEE = BMR × activity factor:** 1.2 sedentary, 1.375 lightly active (1-3 d/wk), 1.55 moderately active (3-5 d/wk), 1.725 very active (6-7 d/wk), 1.9 extremely active (physical job / twice-daily). Getting activity level right matters more than a precise BMR.

**Step 3 — Adjust for goal:** subtract a deficit (fat loss) or add a surplus (gain).

**Step 4 — Set macros in this order:**
1. **Protein first** (g/kg per §7), because it protects lean mass and drives satiety. `g × 4 = kcal`.
2. **Fat next**, as a **% of total calories** (start ~20-35%, often 30%). `kcal × % ÷ 9 = grams`.
3. **Carbs fill the remainder:** `total kcal − protein kcal − fat kcal`, then `÷ 4 = grams`. Carbs are the macro that flexes most with training volume.

*Worked example (from the book):* 100 kg man, moderately active. BMR 1,944 → TDEE 3,014 → 500 deficit → ~2,500 kcal. Protein 1.6 g/kg = 160 g (640 kcal). Fat 30% = 750 kcal (~83 g). Carbs = 2,500 − 640 − 750 = 1,110 kcal (~278 g).

## 12. Fat loss and muscle gain — rates and deficits

- **Fat-loss target: 0.5-1.0% body weight per week (~1-2 lb / 0.4-0.9 kg).** Set **1% BW/week as an upper ceiling** for almost everyone.
- ~**3,500 kcal ≈ 1 lb.** So 1 lb/wk ≈ **500 kcal/day** deficit; 2 lb/wk ≈ **1,000 kcal/day**. Percent-based deficits scale with size (a 325-lb client can safely run a far larger absolute deficit than a 130-lb client).
- **Deficits under ~250 kcal/day are too small** to accumulate meaningfully (can take 4-6 weeks to see 1 real pound, and any tracking error erases them). **Very large deficits are unsustainable and no more effective** — a 30% deficit does no better than a 10% deficit because adherence collapses.
- **Muscle/weight gain:** achievable fast via surplus, but for lean gain aim ~**0.5-1.0% body weight per month** to limit fat accrual. Pair with resistance training and adequate protein.

## 13. Plateaus and maintenance — NASM's central insight

**Adherence outranks diet type.** Across diet-comparison data, people who adhere lose more *regardless of which diet*, and low adherence predicts greater regain at 1-2 years. So a plateau (stalled ≥1 month) is **almost always a lapse in the deficit (usually under-reported intake), not a "broken metabolism."**

Why lapses happen — three buckets:
- **Physiological:** a deficit drives hunger up hard. Appetite rises ~**100 kcal/day for every kg lost**, a drive stronger than any metabolic adaptation, and it **persists ~2 years**. Reduced-obese clients don't feel fullness cues normally, so they inadvertently overeat.
- **Psychological:** varied, individual; needs a client-centered approach, not a template.
- **Environmental:** hyperpalatable food nearby drives spontaneous overeating (up to +54% over maintenance). Teach clients to **engineer their food environment** (keep trigger foods out of sight/the house).

**Breaking a plateau = re-establish the deficit** (lower intake and/or raise expenditure), chosen by client fit:
1. Room to cut + hunger not high → reduce intake (lower energy density, smaller portions, less frequency).
2. Room to cut but high hunger → add activity instead.
3. Intake already low and accurate → add activity (formal, NEAT, or recreational).
4. Intake "low" but under-reporting suspected → *increase* prescribed calories to improve adherence and/or fix self-monitoring, plus add activity.

Reframe plateaus as **maintenance practice** — landings on the staircase where the client rehearses sustaining a lighter body weight. Educate up front that plateaus are near-certain and normal.

## 14. Special populations (within scope, referring when clinical)

- **Older adults (65+):** protein 0.9-1.6 g/kg; resistance training + protein distribution to fight sarcopenia.
- **Athletes:** protein 1.6-2.2 g/kg; carbs scaled to volume (3-12 g/kg/day); intra/post-workout fueling matters. Endurance and physique-extreme athletes (bodybuilding peak, wrestling weight cuts, Ironman) belong with a specialist/CSSD, not a general coach.
- **Pregnant/lactating:** out of scope — refer to OB; only coach under written OB guidance.
- **Chronic disease (T2DM, CVD, etc.):** out of scope to prescribe; support self-management *after* the medical team sets the plan. Even **5-10% body-weight loss** materially improves T2DM. Watch for hypoglycemia signs (hunger, dizziness, sweating, headache, thirst) and report to their physician.
- **Disordered eating / body dysmorphia:** screen informally (SCOFF), never diagnose, refer to an eating-disorder specialist and pause sessions.

## 15. Coach's checklist

1. **Clear the scope gate first.** Screen (health history + SCOFF), and refer anything clinical, disordered, pregnant, or non-progressing. Educate and empower; never prescribe.
2. **Meet the client's stage of change** before planning; premature plans breed resistance.
3. **Coach with MI:** open questions, reflections, affirmations, summaries; evoke change talk; protect autonomy; build self-efficacy by shrinking tasks.
4. **Anchor goals on process/behavior, rooted in the client's values,** not on the scale number alone.
5. **Set intake:** Mifflin BMR × activity factor → adjust for goal → protein first (1.2-2.2 g/kg) → fat (20-35%) → carbs fill the rest.
6. **Fat loss 0.5-1%/wk (ceiling 1%); ~500 kcal/day = 1 lb/wk.** Prefer the smallest effective deficit for adherence.
7. **Protein 20-40 g/meal, ~4 meals; fiber 25 g (W) / 38 g (M); added sugar <10%; hydrate to urine color + body weight.**
8. **Grade supplements by evidence** (creatine, caffeine, protein, EAAs strongest); coach the client to clear specific dosing with an RDN/physician.
9. **Treat every plateau as an adherence + hunger problem first,** re-establish the deficit by the method that fits the client, and reframe plateaus as maintenance practice.
10. **Adherence beats optimization.** The best plan is the one the client will actually keep.
