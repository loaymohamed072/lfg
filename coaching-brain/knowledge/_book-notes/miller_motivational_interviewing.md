# Motivational Interviewing — Coaching Distillation

**Source:** Miller & Rollnick et al., *Motivational Interviewing in the Treatment of Psychological Problems* (2nd Ed., 2015), building on Miller & Rollnick, *Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change* (3rd Ed.). Original paraphrase for a fitness-coaching context. No verbatim text reproduced.

> **Scope note (read first).** MI was built by clinicians for therapy, addiction, and health-behavior work. A fitness coach is not a therapist. Use the *conversational method* here (how to talk about change) and leave the clinical material where it belongs. If a client shows signs of an eating disorder, clinical depression, self-harm, trauma, or substance dependence, your job is to refer to a qualified professional, not to treat. MI helps you coach adherence, habits, and motivation. It does not license you to do mental-health work.

---

## 1. The one idea that matters

People change when *they* say the reasons out loud, not when you say them. The book's central finding: the more a client argues for change during a conversation, the more likely they change; the more they defend the status quo, the less likely. And here's the kicker for coaches, how much of each you hear is largely driven by how *you* talk. You can dial a client's motivation up or down with your own responses.

That reframes the coach's job. You are not the expert installing motivation into an empty client. You are drawing out motivation the client already has. The book's line for the whole method: instead of "I have what you need and I'll give it to you," the stance is "you have what you need, and together we'll find it."

**The trap to avoid (the "righting reflex").** When someone is on the fence and you see the obvious fix, the instinct is to educate, persuade, and push the pro-change side. That instinct backfires. If you argue for change, an ambivalent client will reliably argue the other side ("yeah, but..."). You've now made *them* the one voicing all the reasons to stay the same. People talk themselves *out* of change when you push. Resist the urge to fix, convince, or lecture.

**Ambivalence is normal, not a defect.** Wanting to change and not wanting to, at the same time, is the ordinary human condition, not resistance or laziness. Someone who's ambivalent about training is actually a step ahead of someone who hasn't considered it at all. Your job is to help them resolve the ambivalence toward action, not to shame them for having it.

## 2. The Spirit (PACE) — the mindset, without which the techniques are hollow

The book is emphatic: MI reduced to a bag of tricks doesn't work. The techniques only land inside the right stance. Four parts:

- **Partnership.** A collaboration between two experts: you know training and nutrition; the client is the world expert on their own life, schedule, body, and history. You work *with* them, not *on* them.
- **Acceptance.** Four pieces: honor their **autonomy** (the choice is always theirs, and saying so out loud reduces defensiveness), affirm their **strengths**, give **accurate empathy** (genuinely understand their world), and respect their **absolute worth** as a person regardless of whether they hit the plan.
- **Compassion.** Act in *their* best interest, not yours. Not "I need this client to hit their target so my numbers look good."
- **Evocation.** Call forth what's already in them, their reasons, values, ideas, plans, rather than trying to install what you assume is missing. The opposite of a deficit model.

For a coach: this is the difference between "here's your plan, follow it" and "let's figure out what you can actually sustain and why it matters to you."

## 3. OARS — the four core skills

The everyday moves you use throughout every conversation.

**O — Open questions.** Questions that give the client room, not yes/no or one-word answers. Intake that's just a checklist of closed questions puts the client in a passive "I answer, you diagnose" role. Informal rule from the book: don't ask three questions in a row, even open ones. Questions aren't the main event; reflections are.

**A — Affirmations.** Catch the client doing something right. Recognize every step in the right direction, however small. Affirm real strengths and efforts, not empty praise. Examples: "That took real discipline this week." "Showing up when you were exhausted, that says something about you." You can also ask clients to name their *own* strengths, which builds rapport and lowers their guard.

**R — Reflections (the most-used skill).** You say back what you think they mean. Two levels:
- *Simple reflection*: repeat or lightly rephrase what they said. Useful but limited, can feel robotic if overused.
- *Complex reflection*: make a guess at what they mean but haven't quite said. The book's image: "continue the paragraph", say the next sentence they were about to say. "The evening snacking is the part you can't seem to get a grip on, and it's frustrating because the rest of the day is dialed in."

The target from the book: *the majority of your responses should be reflections, not questions.* This is the hardest skill and the one coaches most underuse. Reflection also gives you built-in feedback: a good reflection makes the client keep talking and reveal more; a miss gets "no, that's not it" and a correction. Either way you learn.

**S — Summaries.** Periodically gather what they've said and hand it back. Shows you're listening, links ideas together, and lets you emphasize the change talk. Think of each bit of change talk as a flower and the summary as the bouquet you're assembling and handing back to them.

## 4. Change talk vs sustain talk — learn to hear it

Everything the client says about the change falls into two buckets.

- **Change talk** = anything that signals movement toward or openness to change. This is the gold. It predicts the client actually doing the thing.
- **Sustain talk** = anything that defends the status quo, staying the same. Normal in anyone ambivalent. Not a character flaw.

You'll hear both, often in one sentence with a "but" in the middle: "I want to get in shape, but I just don't have the time." The skill is tuning your ear so change talk *jumps out* at you the moment it appears, and knowing that of everything a client says, that's the material to grab and grow.

**Timing insight coaches miss:** it's not the change talk at the *start* of a conversation that predicts change, it's the change talk toward the *end*, and the overall shift across the conversation. In a good conversation the balance tips from sustain talk toward change talk as it goes. Your job is to tip it.

### DARN-CAT — the two grades of change talk

**Preparatory change talk (DARN)** — signals leaning toward change without committing yet:
- **D — Desire:** want, wish, like, hope. "I want to feel less winded on the stairs."
- **A — Ability:** can, could, able to. "I could probably meal-prep on Sundays."
- **R — Reasons:** the if-then payoff. "If I lose the weight my knee will stop hurting."
- **N — Need:** must, have to, need to (without necessarily a reason attached). "Something's got to change, I can't keep going like this."

**Mobilizing change talk (CAT)** — signals actual movement toward doing it. This is what you want to hear before you move to planning:
- **C — Commitment:** I will, I promise, I'm going to. The verbs of a real agreement.
- **A — Activation:** I'm willing, I'm ready, I'll consider it. Not quite a promise but leaning in.
- **T — Taking steps:** something they've *already done*. "I bought running shoes." "I filled the prescription." "I did the food diary you asked for." "I went two days without ordering takeout." Treat these as gold and affirm them, they're change in motion.

When you start hearing CAT language, the client is signalling readiness to plan.

## 5. Evoking change talk — how to pull it out

You don't wait for change talk, you invite it. The master principle: **ask open questions whose natural answer is change talk.** Think one step ahead: if I ask this, what will they most likely say?

Compare:
- "What are some good reasons for you to make this change?" → change talk ✓
- "Why haven't you done it yet?" → sustain talk ✗
- "Knowing yourself, how might you actually pull this off?" → change talk ✓
- "What do you like about how things are now?" → sustain talk ✗

Evoking strategies from the book, translated for coaching:

- **Ask directly for DARN.** "What would you like to be different about your fitness?" (Desire) / "If you decided to, how might you do it?" (Ability) / "What are the best reasons to change this?" (Reasons) / "How important is this to you right now?" (Need)
- **Look forward.** "How would you like your health to look a year from now?" / "If nothing changes, where does this go?" / "What's the worst that happens if you keep on as you are?"
- **Explore values.** "What matters most to you?" then connect the goal to it. Tying training to being able to keep up with their kids beats any macro lecture.
- **Ask for elaboration and examples** when change talk appears (see EARS below).

### Scaling questions (steal this daily)

"On a scale of 0 to 10, how important is it to you to [change] right now?" Client says a 4.

Then the move that separates coaches: ask **"why a 4 and not a 0 or 1?"** — the answer is change talk (they tell you *why they care*). **Never** ask "why a 4 and not a 7?" — that pulls sustain talk (they tell you why they can't). Same trick for confidence: "How confident are you, 0 to 10, that you could do this?" then "why that number and not lower?"

## 6. Responding to change talk — EARS

When you hear change talk, don't just nod. Respond in ways that produce *more* of it. EARS is OARS with the E swapped in:

- **E — Elaborate/Example.** "In what ways would you feel better?" "Tell me about a time you did stick to it, what was different?" Curiosity, genuine.
- **A — Affirm.** "That's a strong reason." "Good for you." "You really care about staying healthy."
- **R — Reflect.** Say the change talk back so they hear it again and keep going.
- **S — Summarize.** Collect the accumulated change talk into a bouquet and hand it back.

The mechanism: the client hears their own reasons three times, once when they say it, once when you reflect it, once in your summary. That repetition is the path out of ambivalence, and it's hard to do alone because solo self-talk just ping-pongs between the two sides.

## 7. Sustain talk and discord (formerly "resistance")

The book deliberately dropped the word "resistance", it blamed the client for something the coach was often causing. It splits into two things:

- **Sustain talk** = arguments for the status quo, *about the change target*. "I don't think I need to cut carbs." Normal. Expected. Not a fight.
- **Discord** = friction *in the relationship*, and it usually has "you" in it: "You don't get how hard my schedule is." "You can't tell me what to eat." "You're not listening." Discord means the alliance is strained.

Both predict a lack of change, and both are highly responsive to *your* style. Key line: it takes two to resist. If you're getting a lot of pushback, that's feedback about your approach, not proof the client is difficult.

**How to roll with it (don't argue back):**
- **Reflect sustain talk instead of countering it.** Often, reflecting one side makes the client voice the other. Client: "I don't really think my diet's the problem." You: "From where you sit, the food isn't the issue." Client: "Well... it probably is, honestly, the weekends are a mess." (They argued your side for you.)
- **Emphasize autonomy.** "It's completely your call." Removing the pressure removes the thing they were pushing against.
- **For discord, address the relationship, not the topic.** Apologize, back off, affirm, shift. "I think I got ahead of you there, my mistake. What's actually feeling most doable to you right now?"
- **Don't take up the pro-change side.** The moment you're arguing for the plan and they're arguing against it, you've lost. Let them carry the change argument.

The book's experience: if you run an MI style from the start, you rarely hit much discord at all. Sustain talk stays (it's normal) but fades as change talk grows.

## 8. The Four Processes — the arc of a coaching conversation

MI moves through four processes. They sound linear but they're recursive, you loop back whenever motivation dips.

1. **Engaging** — build the working relationship. OARS, empathy, no agenda-pushing yet. Everything rests on this; if engagement breaks, go back to it.
2. **Focusing** — agree on *what* you're working on. Sometimes the client brings the goal, sometimes the context sets it (they hired a fat-loss coach), sometimes you see a concern they don't share yet. Find a mutually acceptable target.
3. **Evoking** — the process most unique to MI. With a clear focus, draw out *their* motivation for it. This is where you recognize, elicit, and respond to change talk. **Skip this if they're already motivated** — evoking reasons from someone who's already decided just annoys them and can cause dropout. Read the room.
4. **Planning** — build the concrete first step. Signal to start: you hear more CAT (mobilizing) talk and less sustain talk. Test the water with a summary of their change talk plus a key question: "Given all that, what do you think you'll do?" / "What's a good first step for you?"

**Planning stays collaborative.** Don't flip into director mode the second they're ready, that can undo all the motivational progress. The plan should come mostly *from them*. Where they genuinely don't know the how (they want fewer injuries but don't know how to program around it), your expertise is welcome and necessary, but offer it as a guide, not a boss.

### Offering advice the MI way: Elicit–Provide–Elicit

You *are* allowed to give expertise, that's what they're paying for. The book's guardrails:
- **Ask permission first.** "Would it help if I shared what's worked for other people in your spot?" Or offer with autonomy language: "You may or may not agree with this..."
- **Don't tell them what they already know.**
- **Elicit → Provide → Elicit:** ask what they already know / think, offer information in a small chunk, then ask what they make of it. Not a monologue, a back-and-forth. Check in constantly.

## 9. Practical realities the book flags for coaches

- **Motivation oscillates.** It ebbs and flows within a session and across weeks. Change isn't linear. A motivated client this week can be ambivalent next week, that's normal, not backsliding failure.
- **Multiple goals, different readiness.** A client can be fired up about training and completely unwilling to touch their diet. Meet each goal where it actually is. Don't force uniform motivation across everything.
- **Change spills over.** Progress in one area often improves others. A win on training adherence can pull nutrition along with it.
- **MI shines with the ambivalent and the "difficult."** The more oppositional or stuck the person, the bigger MI's edge over just telling them what to do. But MI is *not* for the already-decided, skip evoking and get to planning.
- **The feedback is built in.** You don't need a supervisor to know if you're doing it right. A good reflection → they keep talking and open up. A good evoking question → you hear change talk. Client change talk is your real-time scoreboard.

## 10. Ready-to-paste coaching scripts

**Opening / engaging**
- "Before we dive into any plan, tell me what made you decide to reach out now."
- "What would need to be different for you to feel this was worth it?"

**Focusing**
- "Of everything going on, what feels most important to work on first?"
- "Would it be okay if we spent a few minutes on [X]? Totally your call."

**Evoking (answers = change talk)**
- "What are the best reasons for you to make this change?"
- "How would you like things to look six months from now?"
- "If nothing changes, where do you see this heading?"
- "What worries you about staying where you are?"
- "How does getting fitter connect to what matters most to you?"

**Scaling**
- "0 to 10, how important is this to you right now?" → "Why a [4] and not a 0 or 1?"
- "0 to 10, how confident are you that you could stick to this?" → "What would move you up a point?"

**Responding to change talk (EARS)**
- "Say more about that." / "When has that worked for you before?"
- "That's a strong reason, it clearly matters to you."
- "So part of you really does want this to change."

**Rolling with sustain talk / discord**
- "It's genuinely your decision, I'm not here to push you."
- "You've got real reasons it's hard right now. What, if anything, still makes it worth trying?"
- "I think I jumped ahead of you there, let me back up. What feels doable *to you*?"

**Affirming**
- "You showed up three times this week when you were slammed, that's not nothing."
- "You already figured out a workaround for the travel days, that's smart."

**Moving to planning (after you hear CAT talk)**
- "Given everything you've said, what do you think you want to do?"
- "What's one small first step you're actually willing to take this week?"
- "How will you know it's working?"

**Offering expertise (Elicit–Provide–Elicit)**
- "What have you already tried for this?" → "Can I share a couple of options that tend to work?" → "Which of those feels most like you?"
